What Is Actually Good (And Must Be Protected)
Before the critique, you need to know what is working, because you should not weaken these things in revision.
The accuracy paradox demonstration is the paper's strongest asset. The parallel dual-GT analysis is methodologically elegant, pre-specified, and produces a finding that is visually compelling and conceptually clear. The Sankey diagram and the four-bar grouped chart are genuinely effective. This is publishable.
The mechanistic explanation of GT noise corruption is well-articulated in §5.2. The argument that a noisy GT and a biased AI share the same directional error structure is not just intuitive — you have actually demonstrated it empirically with two independent lines of evidence (radiologist consensus + AI confidence on false-negative images, Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.013). That convergence is strong.
The reliance taxonomy analysis is the paper's most clinically credible section. The finding that 100% of measured over-reliance events under the Platinum GT were false-negative acceptances, with p < 0.0001 on the directional binomial test, is striking and robust.
The writing quality is well above average for this type of paper. The argumentation is mostly coherent and the Discussion does what a Discussion should do.

The Problems That Would Cause Rejection
1. The central psychometric finding is statistically underpowered, and you have told the reviewers so.
This is the most serious issue. You report a post-hoc power calculation for the Neuroticism OR = 1.178 and find 17% power. You correctly call it "substantially underpowered." But then you present it as a "central finding" in the abstract, in the introduction's contribution list, and in the Discussion. A JAMIA reviewer will read §5.6 and §4.4 and ask: how can a finding with 17% power be the paper's central psychometric contribution?
The 17% figure means that if Neuroticism truly has the effect you observed, you had only a 1-in-6 chance of detecting it. The corollary is that what you detected may simply be a false positive inflated by sampling variability — the classic winner's curse in underpowered studies. You partially acknowledge this by calling it "hypothesis-generating," but the framing elsewhere is stronger than that, and the inconsistency will not go unnoticed.
What to do: The paper needs to commit to one of two framings and maintain it consistently. Either this is an exploratory study generating hypotheses about Neuroticism (in which case the abstract cannot call it a "validated predictor"), or you believe the effect is real and you need to present a more robust argument for why the point estimate is reliable despite low power (convergence across multiple analyses, directional consistency across all three KL1 strategies, the theoretical a priori grounding). I would recommend the former framing, executed rigorously. Reviewers respect honesty; they do not respect inconsistency between the abstract and the limitations section.
2. The primary pre-registered hypothesis was null, and the paper pivots away from it without enough transparency.
Conscientiousness was the pre-registered primary predictor. It was null under both ground truths. The paper moves past this in about two sentences. JAMIA reviewers are aware of the garden of forking paths. A reviewer will ask: if Conscientiousness had been significant, would Neuroticism have received the same prominence? The current structure looks like post-hoc elevation of a secondary predictor after the primary one failed — even though Neuroticism was pre-specified.
What to do: Add a short paragraph in the Discussion that explicitly addresses the pre-registration issue. Acknowledge the hierarchy: Conscientiousness was primary and failed. Neuroticism was pre-specified secondary and succeeded — under the corrected GT only. State clearly that the hypothesis hierarchy was set before data collection and that this ordering is documented. If you have a registration record (OSF, ClinicalTrials, etc.), cite it. If you do not, this will be a problem.
3. The stimulus set is 27 images. This is very small for behavioral inference.
The exclusion of 23 KL1 images — completely justified methodologically — leaves you with 27 stimuli. The class imbalance is 21:6. You report balanced accuracy and F1, which is correct, but the issue is not just metric choice. Behavioral estimates derived from 27 images per participant have very wide confidence intervals at the image level. A reviewer will note that the over-reliance rate of 7.8% under the Platinum GT means each participant had, on average, 2.1 over-reliance events out of 27 trials. Individual-level Spearman correlations between Neuroticism and a variable computed from ~2 events are inherently noisy.
What to do: You cannot get more images, but you must be more explicit about what this constraint means for the precision of behavioral estimates. A sentence like "individual-level over-reliance rates were estimated from a small number of qualifying events per participant, which limits the reliability of the behavioral outcome variable and may contribute to the width of the confidence intervals observed" would be honest and pre-emptive.
4. Several 2025–2026 references will raise reviewer flags.
JAMIA reviewers check reference lists. Several of your citations have forms that will prompt scrutiny: "Jackson et al., 2026" (attributed to JAMIA itself), "Vereschak et al., 2026" (JMIR), "Khela et al., 2026," "Langer et al., 2025," "Biro et al., 2025," "Ha et al., 2025," "Omar et al., 2025," "Purington et al., 2024," "Buçinca et al., 2025." Some of these have incomplete citation details (no volume, no pages, no DOI). If any of these are in press, forthcoming, or were generated by a research assistant without verification, a reviewer discovering one fabricated or unverifiable citation will reject on that basis alone, regardless of the science.
What to do: Before submission, verify every single 2024–2026 reference with a live URL or DOI. Remove any citation you cannot independently verify exists in its described form. This is non-negotiable. If some of these are legitimate preprints, label them as such.
5. The DANNY critique risks alienating reviewers who know the DANNY authors.
In §5.5 you write: "We propose that DANNY's specific anomaly is consistent with the suppressor mechanism and warrants direct investigation." The framing is careful, but the surrounding text makes fairly strong implications. You suggest the DANNY study may have failed to observe expected over-reliance rates because of its noisy GT — this is a notable claim about a published study at IUI 2025. You have not contacted the DANNY authors, you have not reanalyzed their data, and you cannot actually demonstrate the suppressor was operating in their study. You are inferring it from a general mechanism.
This is not necessarily wrong scientifically. But JAMIA is a small field. If a reviewer knows the DANNY authors, or is one of them, the framing may feel aggressive rather than collegial. More importantly, the claim exceeds your evidence.
What to do: Soften the specific DANNY claim to a methodological observation rather than a study-specific diagnosis. Something like: "Studies that evaluate annotator behavior against the OAI repository's original labels — including foundational work in this paradigm — may have encountered the suppressor mechanism we document here. Direct empirical investigation using parallel GT validation would be needed to confirm this." That is defensible. Diagnosing DANNY's anomaly by name requires evidence you do not have.
6. The GEE–Wilcoxon discrepancy on the primary outcome is handled well in §4.2, but the abstract and introduction need to reflect the ambiguity honestly.
The abstract states the AI effect was "non-significant" under the Platinum Standard. But the Wilcoxon was significant (p = 0.037). A reviewer reading the abstract will see this discrepancy when they reach the Results and may feel misled. The current choice — anchoring on the GEE and treating Wilcoxon as secondary — is defensible and pre-specified, but the abstract should acknowledge the divergence, not suppress it.
What to do: In the abstract, replace the flat "non-significant" with something like "non-significant in the primary GEE model (p = 0.178) but directionally consistent in the Wilcoxon sensitivity check (p = 0.037), reflecting a marginal, individually heterogeneous benefit." This is more honest and actually more interesting.

Moderate Issues (Major Revision Territory, Not Rejection Triggers)
The cohort is adolescent high school students. This is explicitly justified as replicating the DANNY non-expert paradigm. The justification holds. But JAMIA's readership is clinical informatics professionals, and the external validity question — does this generalize to real annotation workers, citizen scientists, or crowdsourced labelers — is underdeveloped. A paragraph in the Discussion addressing the likely direction and magnitude of differences between adolescents and adult non-expert annotators would strengthen this.
The brittle benefit finding needs to be more carefully separated from the overall Session 2 decline. You note this in §5.4, but the argument that Group 1's collapse is "differential and condition-specific" rests on a comparison to Group 0's session effect — which itself may reflect the general OR = 0.763 decline. Did you test whether the Group 1 Session 2 drop exceeds what the overall model would predict? A formal test of this contrast would make the brittle benefit claim far more defensible.
The mediation analysis result is null but under-interpreted. You report that confidence does not mediate the AI–accuracy relationship (Path A: p = 0.510). This is presented almost in passing. But it is actually an important negative finding with design implications: if AI assistance does not work through increased confidence calibration, then interfaces designed to manipulate confidence (like yours, which displays numerical percentages) may be solving the wrong problem. This deserves more prominence and a sentence connecting it to the practical design implications.
The three "minimum reporting standards" in §5.5 are underdeveloped. These are potentially the most field-consequential output of the paper. Right now they read as three bullet points embedded in a section that also contains the DANNY critique and the mediation finding. They deserve their own subsection — perhaps a small table — with operationalized criteria. If these become a citable standard, the paper's long-term impact increases substantially.

Minor Issues
The subtitle is too long. JAMIA does not use subtitle structures like this — the colon-separated subtitle construction makes it look like a conference paper rather than a journal article.
The ICAR-6 floor effect (M = 1.12/6) is acknowledged in limitations but deserves a sentence in the Methods explaining why ICAR-6 was chosen for an adolescent population if it was known to be normed for adults. Reviewers will read this as a preventable design error (your word) and ask why it was not caught earlier.
The word "terrifying" in §2.4 ("a terrifying quantification of this risk") is inappropriate for a scientific paper and will be flagged.

Summary Verdict
The paper has a genuinely publishable core: the GT noise suppressor mechanism, the accuracy paradox demonstration, and the behavioral reliance taxonomy analysis. These contributions, properly framed, are novel and methodologically rigorous enough for JAMIA.
The paper as submitted has two fatal inconsistencies that will cause rejection: the mismatch between presenting a 17%-powered result as a "central finding" while calling it "hypothesis-generating" in the limitations, and the unverified 2025–2026 reference list. Both are fixable. The DANNY critique needs softening. The brittle benefit analysis needs a formal between-group contrast. The three reporting standards need formalization.
If you address these six items, the paper is competitive. The ideas are there. The execution needs one more serious pass before it is ready.
