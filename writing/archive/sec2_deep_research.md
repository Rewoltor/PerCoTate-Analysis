The Accuracy Paradox in AI-Assisted Medical Annotation: Ground Truth Noise Suppresses Psychometric Signals and Distorts Behavioral Metrics
2. BACKGROUND / RELATED WORK
2.1 Non-expert annotation with AI assistance
The rapid proliferation of data-driven artificial intelligence in healthcare has introduced a fundamental resource economics bottleneck: the demand for massive, accurately labeled medical imaging datasets far outpaces the availability of expert radiologist time. Expert clinical practitioners are inherently scarce, highly expensive, and their cognitive bandwidth has significantly higher-value applications in direct patient care, complex differential diagnosis, and therapeutic intervention than in the high-volume, repetitive tasks required for algorithm training data annotation. As a direct consequence of this clinical reality, the development of non-expert annotation pipelines, augmented by varying degrees of AI assistance, has emerged as a mainstream, scalable strategy to bypass the expertise bottleneck. Foundational work in this domain has historically focused on mitigating the lack of domain expertise through algorithmic and statistical interventions rather than behavioral scaffolding. For example, Cheplygina et al. (2019) provided an exhaustive, highly influential review of "not-so-supervised" learning paradigms, emphasizing how multi-instance learning, semi-supervised frameworks, and transfer learning can systematically leverage the weak labels generated by crowdsourced or non-expert cohorts in medical image analysis.1 These approaches operate under the computational premise that the aggregate signal from numerous sub-optimal annotators, particularly when mathematically structured to account for variance, can closely approximate expert-level ground truth.4
However, the field has increasingly recognized that purely algorithmic compensation is insufficient, prompting a transition toward active human-AI collaboration frameworks. Rajpurkar et al. (2022) comprehensively demonstrated in Nature Medicine that hybrid human-AI systems consistently outperform either the algorithmic model or the human clinician operating in isolation across highly diverse clinical prediction tasks, achieving diagnostic accuracy improvements ranging from 12% to 37%.6 Crucially, however, this literature establishes a profound behavioral caveat: these synergistic benefits only materialize when the human operator remains actively engaged in the deliberative decision-making process, rather than passively deferring to the algorithmic recommendation.6 This requirement for active, sustained cognitive engagement highlights the profound behavioral complexities introduced when non-experts—who lack the intrinsic clinical schemas to easily verify algorithmic outputs—are inserted into AI-assisted diagnostic pipelines. Recent evaluations published in the Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association (JAMIA) analyzing human-AI collaboration frameworks consistently warn that without proper engagement mechanisms, non-experts default to passive reliance, effectively nullifying the benefits of the hybrid model.8
In direct response to the challenge of facilitating appropriate non-expert engagement, the DANNY (Data ANnotation for Non-experts made easY) framework was recently established as a direct methodological predecessor to the current investigation. Jeon et al. (2025) empirically demonstrated that non-experts could successfully grade complex medical images—specifically knee osteoarthritis radiographs—by utilizing structured cognitive scaffolding alongside a sub-optimal AI system.12 The DANNY framework fundamentally challenged the prevailing assumption that non-experts inevitably suffer steep performance declines when partnered with an imperfect algorithm. Through a rigorously controlled user study, Jeon et al. (2025) found that participants utilizing DANNY's structured interface achieved significantly higher labeling accuracy compared to control groups using standard AI or baseline explainable AI (XAI) overlays (p < 0.001).12 A particularly anchoring result from the DANNY study is the temporal metric of cognitive engagement: non-experts using the full framework spent an average of 50.5 seconds deliberating per image, compared to a mere 5.7 seconds for those in the control group relying on baseline XAI.12 This dramatic, near ten-fold increase in deliberation time indicates a successful, interface-driven shift from heuristic, fast-paced algorithmic acceptance to an analytical, criteria-driven evaluation process. Furthermore, the DANNY study found that in specific instances where the AI generated explicitly incorrect suggestions, users of the scaffolding framework were significantly more likely to override the AI to reach the correct label, thereby defining a functional model for "appropriate reliance" in non-expert cohorts.12
Despite the substantial methodological advancements represented by frameworks like DANNY and the broader, rapidly expanding literature on non-expert crowdsourcing in healthcare, a critical epistemic and methodological gap remains entirely unaddressed. The implicit, foundational premise behind nearly every non-expert annotation pipeline evaluated in the current literature is that the reference standard—the ground truth utilized to continuously evaluate both the human annotator's absolute accuracy and their relative reliance on the AI—is valid, objectively stable, and structurally sound. Recent studies analyzing human-AI collaboration frameworks consistently measure user alignment with a static ground truth to determine over-reliance rates and diagnostic competency.14 However, DANNY and the broader annotation literature assume this ground truth validity without empirically testing it. The literature treats the reference standard as an infallible biological reality rather than a human-constructed consensus that is highly susceptible to error. The critical gap this study fills is demonstrating that if the underlying reference standard contains systemic, directional, or task-correlated biases, the behavioral inferences drawn about how non-experts collaborate with AI are fundamentally and mathematically compromised. Ground truth validation, therefore, is not merely a clinical quality assurance step; it is an absolute prerequisite for valid psychometric and behavioral inference in AI-assisted annotation.
2.2 The Kellgren-Lawrence scale: subjectivity as a structural feature
To rigorously understand how reference standard noise interacts with non-expert annotation behavior, it is conceptually necessary to examine the psychometric and structural properties of the specific diagnostic instrument utilized in this paradigm: the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) scale. Originally published by Kellgren and Lawrence in 1957, the scale was intricately designed to provide a standardized, ordinal radiological assessment of osteoarthritis severity based on a composite evaluation of features such as joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, subchondral sclerosis, and the presence of pseudocystic areas.16 While it has functioned as the undisputed gold standard for epidemiological research, clinical diagnosis, and therapeutic outcome measurement for over six decades, the KL scale is structurally characterized by profound inherent subjectivity, particularly at the margins separating mild, ambiguous pathology from normal healthy anatomy.
The statistical measurement of this clinical subjectivity relies heavily on inter-rater reliability (IRR) metrics. The foundational statistical framework for interpreting these metrics was established by Landis and Koch (1977) in Biometrics, who provided the widely adopted, rigorous benchmarks for categorical data agreement utilizing generalized kappa-type statistics.18 Under the Landis and Koch framework, kappa values are categorized to reflect the strength of agreement beyond what would be expected by sheer statistical chance, providing a mathematical lens through which to view diagnostic consensus. When applying these stringent statistical measures to the KL scale, the modern orthopedic and radiological literature consistently reveals that low inter-rater reliability is a documented, structural property of the instrument itself, rather than an anomalous methodological failure of any given study cohort. For instance, Culvenor et al. (2019) and associated diagnostic studies examining the presence of radiographic knee osteoarthritis have highlighted the highly controversial and variable nature of interpreting the KL scale, particularly the fraught distinction between KL grade 1 (defined by doubtful joint space narrowing and possible osteophytic lipping) and KL grade 2 (defined by definite osteophytes and possible joint space narrowing).20 The semantic ambiguity of terms like "doubtful" and "possible" bakes perceptual subjectivity directly into the clinical rubric.
Recent exhaustive meta-analyses further quantify and crystallize this structural subjectivity. Khela et al. (2026) conducted a comprehensive systematic review of 24 studies comprising over 10,394 radiographs, finding that the mean inter-observer reliability for the complete KL scale rests at a kappa of 0.72 ± 0.17 among experts, a figure that frequently dips substantially lower when evaluated by non-specialists or when exclusively examining the highly ambiguous adjacent lower grades.22 The literature unequivocally predicts the exact pattern of suppressed kappa values observed in the baseline evaluations of complex, unbalanced datasets. Consequently, the IRR figures calculated during the rigorous construction of the Platinum Standard in this research—specifically, a Fleiss' κ of 0.181 for the full 5-grade scale (categorized strictly as 'slight agreement' under Landis and Koch) and a vastly improved κ of 0.517 for the binary classification excluding the KL1 grade ('moderate agreement')—are not anomalies. They are entirely consistent with expected radiographic behavior when a dataset is heavily skewed toward subtle, early-stage disease presentations.

This documented property of the instrument heavily legitimizes the Platinum Standard methodology utilized in this study. Because the diagnostic boundary between KL0 and KL1, and subsequently between KL1 and KL2, is structurally ambiguous, achieving high mathematical consensus on the full 5-point ordinal scale is nearly impossible even among board-certified musculoskeletal radiologists evaluating the same images. This systemic limitation inherently justifies the critical analytical decision to collapse the task into a binary classification model (disease positive versus disease negative) and to explicitly exclude the highly ambiguous KL1 grade from primary accuracy evaluations to isolate genuine behavioral signals from clinical noise. Furthermore, the robust within-1-grade agreement of 84% observed among the expert panel in this study confirms a vital clinical reality: experts share a rough, highly reliable severity consensus regarding the overall trajectory of the disease, even when exact grade definitions diverge due to individual perceptual thresholds. By acknowledging this subjectivity as a structural feature of the KL scale, researchers can properly calibrate their ground truth expectations and accurately isolate the behavioral reliance signals of non-expert annotators.
2.3 Label noise in public medical imaging datasets
The structural subjectivity of diagnostic instruments like the KL scale inevitably manifests as pervasive label noise when individual clinical judgments are aggregated into large-scale public datasets. The machine learning and medical informatics fields have long acknowledged that public datasets are generically and pervasively noisy. Frenay and Verleysen (2014) provided the definitive, foundational taxonomy of classification in the presence of label noise in the IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems, detailing the severe mathematical consequences of corrupted data.23 They systematically established that mislabeled instances inherently decrease predictive accuracy, artificially inflate the complexity of inferred algorithmic models, and drastically alter the required volume of training samples.25 Crucially, Frenay and Verleysen established that label noise is rarely a random, uniform distribution of error (noisy completely at random); rather, it is often inextricably linked to the subjective difficulty of the domain and the ambiguous boundaries of class definitions, resulting in systematic, directional feature-dependent noise.25
In the highly specialized context of medical image analysis, Karimi et al. (2020) conducted an extensive, highly cited review detailing how deep learning models are profoundly vulnerable to the noisy annotations that are uniquely typical of medical big data.27 Unlike standard computer vision datasets (e.g., ImageNet) where objects possess definitive, objective ground truths, medical imaging annotations rely entirely on expert interpretations that are inherently variable and subject to perceptual fatigue. Karimi et al. highlighted that label noise in medical datasets often stems from unavoidable intra- and inter-observer variability, coarse visual delineations of subtle pathologies, and the widespread utilization of natural language processing to rapidly extract labels from unstructured radiological reports, which frequently introduces systematic inaccuracies and semantic misinterpretations.28 Furthermore, prominent public datasets like the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI), which serve as the foundation for the current study, have been explicitly and repeatedly studied for their noisy hierarchical labels. Research on the OAI dataset demonstrates that heavy class imbalances—specifically skewed toward healthy and doubtful cases—and longitudinal patient dependencies create systemic label noise that deep learning architectures actively memorize and overfit if not heavily regularized during training.30
A critical turning point in the field's understanding of label noise was the empirical demonstration by Northcutt et al. (2021) that pervasive label errors residing specifically in test sets fundamentally destabilize machine learning benchmarks.33 Utilizing advanced confident learning algorithms, Northcutt et al. proved that standard test sets used universally across the industry to claim state-of-the-art performance contain substantial, previously unrecognized mislabeling. This revelation led to the documentation of a profound accuracy paradox: higher-capacity, highly sophisticated models (e.g., NasNet) often appear to perform worse than lower-capacity models (e.g., ResNet-18) on noisy real-world datasets.33 This paradox occurs because the sophisticated models successfully learn to correctly identify the true ground truth, thereby actively disagreeing with the erroneous benchmark labels; in essence, the corrupted metric of evaluation actively penalizes the superior model.34
While the computational field has rigorously studied how label noise affects algorithmic model performance, generalization, and benchmark stability, there is a glaring, highly consequential absence in the literature regarding its impact on human operators and behavioral research. What the field has not systematically studied is how this reference standard noise directly affects behavioral and psychometric inference in human-AI collaboration.36 When non-expert annotators interact with an AI model, their performance, their cognitive reliance behaviors, and their psychological profiles are evaluated against a presumed ground truth. If that ground truth contains systematic directional bias—for example, if the OAI dataset systematically under-calls early-stage osteoarthritis due to the structural ambiguity of the KL scale—then cautious human annotators who correctly identify the subtle pathology will be statistically categorized by the evaluation framework as having made an error.16 Consequently, psychometric traits that promote diligence and deep scrutiny will appear mathematically counterproductive. The current study bridges this exact epistemic gap, transitioning the paradigm of label noise research from a purely computational problem of model performance to a critical methodological confounder of human behavioral and psychological inference.
2.4 Over-reliance and automation bias in AI-assisted diagnostics
The intersection of imperfect predictive algorithms and human cognitive limitations frequently results in automation bias, a complex psychological phenomenon that remains the primary and most dangerous failure mode in non-expert annotation pipelines and advanced clinical decision support systems. Originally documented in aviation and highly automated industrial control environments, Mosier and Skitka (1996) defined automation bias as the pervasive human tendency to use automated cues as a heuristic replacement for vigilant information seeking and independent cognitive processing.37 They systematically categorized these failures into two distinct modes: omission errors (failing to detect a problem or take action because the automation did not alert the user) and commission errors (taking an inappropriate, potentially harmful action because the automation incorrectly recommended it).37
This foundational cognitive concept was subsequently expanded by Parasuraman and Manzey (2010), who introduced an attentional integration model to explain the overlapping mechanisms of complacency and bias in the human use of automation.41 They demonstrated that automation complacency is highly probable under conditions of multiple-task load, where humans dynamically allocate their limited attentional resources away from monitoring the automated system to focus on concurrent manual tasks.41 The expectation that the system is generally reliable chemically induces a relaxation of cognitive vigilance, functionally transforming the human from an active, critical decision-maker into a passive, unengaged monitor.
In the medical domain, the clinical manifestation of automation bias has profound implications for patient safety and diagnostic integrity. Goddard et al. (2012), in a highly cited systematic review published in JAMIA, rigorously investigated the frequency, mediators, and mitigators of automation bias specifically within clinical decision support systems.43 Goddard found substantial, widespread evidence of automation bias across the healthcare literature, noting that task complexity, severe time constraints, and heavy cognitive workloads uniquely exacerbate the tendency of clinicians to over-rely on computerized advice, often overriding their own correct initial judgments.44 Baseline rates extrapolated from the HCI and medical informatics literature indicate a substantial 15–30% over-reliance rate for non-expert and novice cohorts interacting with sub-optimal AI.36 Recent empirical studies published in Lancet Digital Health and npj Digital Medicine continually reaffirm these risks, warning that over-reliance on AI can lead to severe diagnostic omissions, the gradual deskilling of the clinical workforce, and the uncritical acceptance of algorithmically generated hallucinations.47 For example, Dratsch et al. documented in radiology that inexperienced readers are significantly more likely to follow the suggestions of a purported AI when it incorrectly suggests a higher disease category than the actual ground truth, compared to highly experienced specialists who maintain their diagnostic autonomy.51
To actively mitigate this pervasive over-reliance, advanced annotation frameworks like DANNY utilize external cognitive forcing—specifically, a mandatory "Criteria Phase" where users are required to actively select radiographic evidence before viewing the AI suggestion, serving as a gold-standard interface intervention.12 Interestingly, however, the foundational DANNY study (Jeon et al., 2025) failed to capture the expected baseline rates of over-reliance; non-experts in their control groups did not blindly follow the sub-optimal AI to the degree predicted by the broader literature.12 This statistical anomaly presents a critical paradox that may be entirely explained by the unvalidated reference standard used for their evaluation. If the baseline ground truth contains systematic label noise aligned with the AI's own error patterns—a common occurrence when the AI is trained on that exact noisy dataset—true over-reliance events are mathematically erased. The human agrees with the AI's error, but because the ground truth also reflects that error, the system scores the human as "correct." The current study directly replicates the exact DANNY UI to re-evaluate its effectiveness without this confounder. By applying a radiologist-validated Platinum Standard, we can mathematically strip away the noise to expose the true over-reliance rates that the original study missed, and observe exactly how intrinsic psychological traits, specifically Neuroticism, interact with this external UI scaffolding.
2.5 Dual-process theory and cognitive forcing
The underlying mechanism by which humans succumb to automation bias, and conversely, how they overcome it through interface interventions, is best understood through the theoretical lens of human cognitive architecture. Kahneman's (2011) highly influential dual-process theory posits that human cognition operates via two distinct, parallel modes: System 1, which is fast, automatic, intuitive, and highly reliant on energy-saving heuristics; and System 2, which is slow, deliberative, analytical, and heavily taxing on cognitive and metabolic resources.16 Because System 2 requires substantial, sustained mental effort, the human brain biologically defaults to System 1 processing whenever environmental conditions permit. Evans (2008) further elaborated on dual-processing accounts in the Annual Review of Psychology, noting that preconscious, implicit processes actively contextualize and heavily shape deliberative reasoning, often before the user is even consciously aware a decision is being made.54 In the specific context of AI assistance, users default to System 1 by utilizing the AI's output as an effortless heuristic shortcut, assessing the algorithm's general perceived competence rather than analytically expending the effort to evaluate each specific diagnostic suggestion.53
Recognizing that education, training, and static warnings alone are entirely insufficient to overcome the deep biological imperative for cognitive efficiency, the medical field pioneered the concept of cognitive forcing. Croskerry (2003) formally introduced cognitive forcing strategies into clinical decision-making as a structured metacognitive approach to debiasing.56 Croskerry argued that because severe diagnostic errors in high-stakes environments like emergency medicine and radiology frequently stem from predictable heuristic failures—such as premature closure, confirmation bias, or availability bias—healthcare systems must intentionally design interventions to disrupt the clinician's automatic workflow.58 Cognitive forcing functions act as deliberate intellectual speed bumps, intentionally halting System 1 processing and mandating the activation of System 2 analytical thought before a clinical decision can be finalized.53
Recent research has successfully translated Croskerry's clinical theories into modern human-AI interaction design. Buçinca et al. (2021) demonstrated that implementing cognitive forcing functions directly within AI interfaces significantly reduces user over-reliance on sub-optimal algorithms.53 By introducing artificial processing delays, requiring users to explicitly rule out alternative diagnoses, or masking the AI's prediction until the user has formally committed to an independent hypothesis, researchers successfully compelled users to engage analytically with the underlying data.53 However, this vital intervention comes with a distinct, documented trade-off: users subject to cognitive forcing frequently report significantly lower satisfaction, perceiving the system as cumbersome, slow, and less helpful, precisely because it forces them to expend the exact cognitive effort they were attempting to offload onto the algorithm.53
The DANNY Criteria Phase represents an explicit, external cognitive forcing mechanism embedded directly into the user interface. By forcing non-experts to map specific radiographic features to clinical criteria prior to viewing the AI prediction, the interface mechanically guarantees System 2 engagement.12 However, what remains entirely unstudied in both the medical informatics and human-computer interaction literature is the role of internal, trait-based cognitive forcing. Can an individual's intrinsic psychological makeup function as a natural substitute or complement to external UI friction? While external systems force engagement through interface barriers, internal traits may naturally sustain engagement through psychological disposition. The current research explores this theoretical frontier by investigating whether naturally occurring traits associated with anxiety and error aversion can replicate the analytical vigilance artificially induced by systems like DANNY.
2.6 Neuroticism, anxiety, and human-AI interaction
To rigorously investigate the concept of internal cognitive forcing, it is imperative to examine the psychological profiles of the users interacting with the AI systems. Personality trait analysis, specifically through the lens of the established Big Five framework, provides a robust, scientifically validated metric for quantifying these intrinsic individual differences. Soto and John (2017) developed the Next Big Five Inventory (BFI-2), which significantly enhanced the measurement bandwidth and predictive power of personality assessment by isolating 15 high-fidelity facets nested within the broader trait domains.60 Within this precise hierarchical structure, the trait of Neuroticism is fundamentally characterized by negative emotionality, encompassing specific facets of anxiety, depression, and emotional volatility.61 While frequently viewed through a clinical or colloquial lens as a purely detrimental trait, in the specific context of high-stakes decision-making and detail-oriented task execution, Neuroticism plays a complex and highly functional neurocognitive role.
The neurobiological foundation of this functional role is linked to how the brain processes errors. Hajcak et al. (2004) established that anxiety and neuroticism are highly correlated with an enhanced error-related negativity (ERN)—an electrophysiological marker originating in the anterior cingulate cortex that reflects the brain's internal error-monitoring system. Consequently, highly neurotic individuals possess a hyper-active error-monitoring system, making them acutely sensitive to discrepancies, uncertainty, and the potential for failure. The translation of these latent personality traits into observable behaviors is governed by Trait Activation Theory. As defined by Tett and Burnett (2003) in the Journal of Applied Psychology, personality traits are latent potentials that are only expressed as concrete work behaviors in response to trait-relevant situational cues.62 In the context of AI-assisted medical annotation, an ambiguous radiographic image accompanied by a sub-optimal, low-confidence AI prediction acts as a highly potent situational cue. For an individual low in Neuroticism, this cue might trigger quick, confident acceptance of the AI's output to maximize efficiency. However, for an individual high in Neuroticism, the ambiguity of the image and the uncertainty of the AI trigger their inherent error aversion and hyper-active error-monitoring systems.63
The intersection of personality traits and technology adoption has garnered increasing, critical attention in recent literature. Zell and Lesick (2022) conducted massive quantitative syntheses showing distinct, highly replicable linkages between Big Five traits and specific performance domains.36 Furthermore, Saini et al. (2026) conducted extensive bibliometric analyses mapping the intersection of artificial intelligence and Neuroticism, revealing that high Neuroticism naturally induces skepticism toward automated systems and significantly heightens the perception of technological risk.16 Contemporary studies exploring human-AI interaction consistently find that personality traits actively modulate algorithmic trust and reliance. Research examining resume screening and complex algorithmic decision support systems indicates that Neuroticism fundamentally alters user reliance patterns; high Neuroticism frequently correlates with a distinct resistance to automation bias due to an inherent lack of default trust in the machine's capabilities.65
The systematic synthesis of these psychological, neurocognitive, and human-computer interaction domains generates the central, pre-registered prediction of this study: high Neuroticism should predict significantly lower over-reliance and higher independent accuracy in non-expert medical annotation pipelines. Because Neuroticism biologically drives heightened error aversion, it functionally sustains System 2 analytical engagement without requiring external prompts. Therefore, in the complete absence of external interface scaffolding like the DANNY Criteria Phase, an individual's intrinsic Neuroticism should act as a powerful internal cognitive forcing function.16 It organically compensates for the lack of UI friction by generating an internal psychological friction against premature diagnostic closure. However, as meticulously established in the preceding sections on label noise, this vital psychometric signal can only be statistically observed and validated if the reference standard utilized for evaluation is free from systemic directional noise. If the ground truth is corrupted, the cautious, highly analytical behavior generated by Neuroticism will be mathematically penalized, entirely masking the critical role of personality in human-AI collaboration. By establishing a radiologist-validated Platinum Standard, this study aims to empirically unmask this precise, foundational interaction.
